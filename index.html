An authentic farm experience

If commercialized farming, gift shops, or the chaos of "farm entertainment" is not your cup of tea, our farm may be just what you're looking for. It's all about our crops, the goings on of a real farm, a keen interest in food, plus the peace and quiet of the country. Kids with imaginations will not want to leave.

Before you pick

Who can pick?

Anyone! Children of all ages are allowed in the patch. Only humans are allowed in the patch.

What do I bring?

Containers: any container will do: baskets, pails, Tupperware, ice cream containers, mixing bowls, etc. We do have containers available, but you have to buy them. Cardboard containers get soggy if it has rained. Raspberries squish under their own weight, so bring lots of containers and only fill them up a bit (no more than 2 inches).

Insect repellent may be needed (mostly in the morning in the raspberry patch), and some refreshment (we do sell water, but feel free to bring your own).

What should I wear?

Wide brimmed hats, old clothes and comfortable shoes. For raspberries, a long sleeved shirt protects your arms from the canes. Be prepared for wet feet in the raspberry patch in the morning; we can get heavy dews. High heels and white pants are not recommended!

When should I come?

Avoid the mid-afternoon; it can get very hot out in the patch. Come first thing in the morning (we open at 9:00), when the berries are cool. Raspberries shouldn't be picked when they're wet; if they are, use them right away.

We keep strict hours. If you arrive before 9:00 you will have to wait to start picking, and access to the patch closes 1 hour prior to closing.

What varieties do you grow?

Raspberries: Prelude, Nova.
Currants: Stikine, Tahsis, Red Lake, Rovada, Hron.

Any other tips?

For fast picking, leave your container on the ground and pick with two hands! Don't pick unripe raspberries; they do not ripen. If you need to pull them off, they are not ripe.

Flowers & herbs

Pots, planters & hanging baskets

Annuals and perennials, herbs and tomato plants: each year we grow hundreds of different cultivars in our greenhouses. We grow them big and beautiful, and we select varieties that will grow well in your containers and gardens, so your garden looks good the day you plant it.

When you come to the farm, you'll explore our production greenhouses and choose flowers right from where we grow them. Warning: our greenhouses are breathtakingly beautiful. You will probably buy more than you planned on, and still come back for more.

From mid-July to mid-October we also cut fresh flower bunches, picked at dawn.

Know your grower

Since 1942

John Hutchinson planted his first strawberry patch in 1942 on his family farm along Lakeshore Road in Oakville. He was 14, and with many men overseas, it was up to John and his mother to run the farm.

In the 1970s he bought the Readhead Farm on Walkers Line, where Hutchinson Farm is today. In the early '80s, Barbara MacTavish set up a table of fresh picked produce along the driveway, and her son David began selling at the Burlington Farmer's Market. Before long the farm left wholesale behind; now everything is sold directly to you.

David MacTavish has worked full time on the farm since 1992 and lives here with his family. Simply awesome produce, fresh picked daily, all grown at our farm in North Burlington: this is what it truly means to be local!

Read the full history

Memorable early crops included tomatoes, which John sold to the now closed Canada Canners plant in Burlington. One year he was their second largest grower; he would have been the largest, but he forgot to pick an entire field!

In the 1960s the Oakville farm was bought by developers, and he moved to the north-east corner of Walkers Line and Britannia Road, where he grew greenhouse tomatoes and cucumbers in what was then one of the largest greenhouses in Ontario.

In the 1970s he bought the Readhead Farm just across the road. He still grew strawberries and tomatoes, but also a bit of everything else, from acorn squash to zucchini. The strawberries were pick-your-own; everything else was sold wholesale.

In the early '80s, OMAFRA crops consultant Joe Uyenaka told him, "John, you're a great plant grower, why don't you use your greenhouse space to grow flowers like geraniums?" Geraniums and other annuals are now the most important crop grown on the farm.

John also met his second wife, Barbara MacTavish, who set up the driveway produce table. It quickly became apparent you didn't have to sell many zucchini directly to the customer to exceed what the farm earned selling wholesale.

Work with us

From seed to plate, work with us growing, selling and eating the crops grown at Hutchinson Farm. Our season runs from March to Halloween, and everyone does a bit of everything: planting, picking, crop care and working the farm store.

  • Greenhouse Groupie: planting greenhouse crops in March and April, Wed–Fri.
  • Crop Crew: picking and tending crops throughout the season.
  • Bouquet Boss: cutting and bunching flowers at dawn, mid-July to mid-October.
  • Store Staff: May to October, usually 8–1 or 1–6 shifts.

All employees work some weekends. The work is outdoors and physically demanding. There is no public transit to the farm, so you'll need a ride (or a 20 minute bike ride from Dundas and Walkers).
